Urban Flood Evacuation Scenario — Synthetic Case Study
Purpose: This document is designed as seed/context material for MiroFish Community. It provides a structured
fictional community, stakeholder network, emergency scenario, alternative decisions, behavioural conditions and
research variables for multi-agent social decision simulation.
Important: All population figures, stakeholder descriptions, relationships, events and conditions in this document are synthetic
assumptions created for research experimentation. They do not represent actual data about any real Mumbai community.

2. Flood Scenario and Timeline
Harbour View Community receives a warning that intense rainfall is expected to continue. Water begins accumulating
on several low-lying roads. As conditions deteriorate, residents must decide whether to evacuate, wait for additional
information, assist neighbours, protect property or seek alternative routes.

3. Stakeholder Groups

The virtual community should not behave as a homogeneous population. Agents should represent different priorities,
constraints, beliefs, relationships and levels of influence.
General residents: Prioritize personal and household safety, reliable information and convenient evacuation.
Parents and caregivers: Prioritize children and dependents; may delay evacuation until family members are
together.
Elderly residents: May have reduced mobility and greater dependence on assistance or accessible transport.
Vulnerable / medically dependent residents: May require medication, medical support, accessible transport and
appropriate shelter.
Students and young adults: Generally more mobile and potentially active in peer-to-peer information sharing.
Shopkeepers and small businesses: May worry about inventory, property security, income loss and evacuation
timing.
Daily-wage / informal workers: May face stronger economic pressure to continue working despite rising risk.
Emergency responders: Prioritize rescue, route clearance, vulnerable households and efficient resource
allocation.
Local officials: Coordinate warnings, evacuation instructions, shelters, transport and public communication.
Healthcare workers: Prioritize patients, medical supplies and continuity of essential services.
Community volunteers: Help distribute information, identify vulnerable households and support evacuation.
Transport operators: Balance passenger safety, route conditions and service availability.
4. Community Relationships and Information Network
Agents are connected through households, neighbourhood ties, workplaces, schools, businesses, community
organizations and online communication. These relationships determine how information travels and how individual
decisions can influence others.
Family → shared decisions, responsibility and mutual assistance.
Neighbours → local observations, evacuation assistance and informal warnings.
Students / peers → rapid peer-to-peer information diffusion.
Shopkeepers ↔ residents → local information and economic dependencies.
Officials → residents → official warnings and evacuation instructions.
Emergency responders → officials → operational information and resource constraints.
Volunteers → vulnerable residents → assistance and local information.
Healthcare workers → vulnerable residents → medical guidance and support.
Social networks → community → rumours, verification, support and behavioural influence.
5. Trust and Information Conditions
Not all agents trust the same information source. Some residents strongly trust official alerts, while others rely on
family members, neighbours, local leaders or direct observation. Information may arrive at different times and may
sometimes conflict.
Official messages are generally considered authoritative but may be perceived as delayed.
Neighbour reports may be fast but incomplete.
Family members can strongly influence individual decisions.
Social-media-style messages may spread quickly and can contain both accurate information and rumours.

6. Flood-Related Constraints
Some roads become difficult or impossible for vehicles to use.
Emergency responders have limited vehicles, personnel and time.
Shelter capacity is smaller than the total community population.
Transport availability decreases as flood conditions worsen.
Some households contain members who cannot evacuate independently.
Residents may be reluctant to leave property unattended.
Businesses may resist early evacuation because of economic losses.
Communication quality differs between highly connected and isolated residents.
Conflicting information may create hesitation, panic or disagreement.

11. Experimental Design
For comparison, the community context and stakeholder-generation process should remain as consistent as possible
while the candidate decision changes. Each scenario can be simulated multiple times with different stochastic
conditions. Results should be reported as distributions or ranges where appropriate rather than relying on a single
run.
Example: Scenario A → repeated simulation runs → social impact metrics → Scenario B → repeated runs → metrics
→ Scenario C → repeated runs → metrics → Scenario D → repeated runs → metrics → comparative analysis.

13. Interpretation Guidelines
The simulation represents a virtual community, not the actual population. Its outputs should be treated as possible
outcomes under specified assumptions. A strategy receiving stronger simulated acceptance does not automatically
mean it will succeed in the real world.
The research should distinguish between simulation outcome, comparative scenario result and real-world
evidence. Real-world deployment would require empirical data, calibration, validation and human oversight.
